Drive Sync · redesign

A quieter Drive Sync

Newest is 12a the light theme, just below, then 11a Notifications, then 10a the menu bar, then 9a Onboarding, then 8a Settings, then 7a Activity — which replaces the chart-led 6a, kept underneath for comparison — then 5a Plan a sync, 4a Deletions and 3a Conflicts. They all build on the settled main-screen direction, 2a Meridian — one window that says nothing at rest and grows a seam between your two sides when traffic starts. The first round — 1a spatial, 1b temporal, 1c almost nothing at all — is kept at the bottom for reference; 2a is 1a and 1c recognised as the same design at two volumes.

All of them keep the brand hexagon and the engine's colour contract, all come with a compact companion so the everyday check-in never needs the full window, and none of them keep the activity grid — it was a table of things you already knew about, sitting in the most valuable space on the screen.

12Light theme
12aThe same system, lit from the frontnot an inversion

Flipping the neutrals would break the whole colour contract. The dark palette's accents are luminous — they work because they glow against near-black. On white the same values read as pastel and lose their meaning, so light uses the darker end of each ramp: burnt orange instead of amber, teal instead of sky, crimson instead of coral. Same three meanings, re-pitched.

Two things transfer unchanged. Maximum contrast is the primary action — white button on dark, near-black button on light. And no colour still means settled: a light screen at rest is warm white and one grey hexagon, which turns out to be the calmest state in either theme.

The surface is a warm off-white, not #fff — pure white makes the burnt orange look muddy and leaves nowhere for a raised card to go. Cards are white, so depth reads as light rather than shadow.

11Notifications — the interrupting surface
11aWhat's worth interrupting forfour notifications, and a long silent list

A sync app has an obvious failure mode: it knows about hundreds of events a day and can tell you about all of them. Then you turn notifications off, and the one that mattered — a folder is about to be deleted from your computer — never arrives. So the design here is mostly a list of things that deliberately stay silent.

The test each one has to pass: would this person want to stop what they're doing? Four events pass. Everything else — every sync pass, every file, every scheduled sweep, every skipped socket — belongs in 7a, where you go looking for it.

One hard rule: nothing destructive is ever a notification button. A banner can offer Keep, because keeping is reversible and safe — but never Delete. Irreversible things happen in a window where you can see what you're losing, not in a corner of the screen you might swat at.

10The tray — where the app actually lives
10aSystem trayglyph + panel, all five states

This is the only part of the app most people see most days, and today it's a text menu — Sync now (3 pending), Resolve 1 conflict — where the label is the whole status report. So the menu becomes the compact panel from 2a: the same hexagon, the same seam, the same sentence. Clicking the glyph shows you the state; it doesn't make you read a list of verbs.

The glyph is the hexagon at 16px, and it has to survive being tiny and monochrome in a crowded top bar — so state is carried by fill, not colour: hollow when settled, a travelling segment while syncing, solid dot when you're needed. Colour is a bonus where the desktop allows it, never the only signal.

One deliberate keep from today's menu: "Close window — keeps syncing". That sentence prevents the single worst misunderstanding a tray app can cause, and it stays spelled out.

9Onboarding — the first sync
9aSetting upthe seam, being built

Onboarding is where the seam gets assembled — you pick a side, then the other, then watch them join. By the time the app is running you already know what the two halves of every later screen mean, without a tour.

Today's wizard has four steps, and the fourth — Start service — exists for the daemon, not the person. It's gone. The CLI check becomes a precondition the app resolves silently unless it fails, so the first thing anyone sees is choosing their folders, not a dependency audit.

The review step's job is one specific reassurance: nothing gets deleted today. So instead of five counters and a scary acknowledgement checkbox, the plan is stated in three sentences with zero destructive actions shown as an explicit, positive fact. The two-way-deletion consent — genuinely important — moves to where it's actually true: after the merge, as the last thing you agree to, rather than buried under a wall of numbers on step 3.

7Activity, rebuilt for the quiet day
7aActivityreplaces 6a · no chart

You're right, and the tide had the same flaw as the grid it replaced: it was beautiful when busy and meaningless the other 95% of the time. A chart of nothing is worse than no chart. And the seam takes over the reassurance job: both sides, counted, agreeing — which is meaningful at zero in a way a flat line never is.

4Deletions — the only screen that destroys things
4aWaiting to be deleteddirection decides severity

The engine's two deletion directions are not equally dangerous and today's UI presents them identically. Deleting on Proton moves the file to Proton's Trash — recoverable. Deleting locally removes it from disk with no trash — gone. So the seam sorts them: left column is permanent, right column is recoverable, and only the left one carries solid red and a typed confirmation.

Each row leads with what you'd lose — name, size, when you last touched it — not with a path and an entity type. Keep is the default action and needs no confirmation; it's the one you can always take back. There is no "approve all" across both columns, because the two columns don't mean the same thing.
